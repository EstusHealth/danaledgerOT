01

The big shift: business owner ≠ clinician

You said it yourself: "I've got a million things I want to achieve and I'm achieving none of them." The flexibility you've always seen as a perk isn't doing you any favours right now. The fix starts with separating two jobs that are currently tangled together: being the clinician and running the business.

  • Creating the best clinicians, not being the best one. My mindset change last year was moving from role-modelling the best clinician to building the system that creates them. I'm now the lowest-billing clinician on my own team, and the team outperforms me on welcome calls, conversions, retention and reports.
  • Service businesses hit one of two limits. You're either demand-constrained (not enough referrals) or supply-constrained (not enough clinicians to fill them). Know which one you're facing, solve it, then solve the next one.
  • Earn your margin. If you grow a team, the clinicians stay when the infrastructure makes their job easy: templates ready, onboarding done, referrals arriving. That's the level of service to aim for.
"From role-modelling the best clinician, to creating the best clinicians."The mindset shift

Why this group works for you

A lot of these clients want, first and foremost, to feel heard, and you're genuinely good at that. As one stream in a busy caseload (alongside NDIS and driving), it works really well.

Watch out for

As your whole caseload it gets fatiguing, especially if you're solutions-focused. Keep it as one block of your week, and do telehealth blocks and driving blocks separately.

02

Your niche: the focus coach

Frame your role as a focus coach for late-diagnosed women with chronic, invisible conditions. You're already on the right track; this gives it language.

  • Open loops → cognitive residue. These women carry so many unresolved things that each one leaves a little residue.
  • Go to war on multitasking. Divided attention means nobody gets their full attention, whether it's the work or the people around them, and frustration grows in every direction.
  • The performance gap. There's dissonance between where they want to be performing and where they currently are. The guilt from that gap is huge. In your words: "It's the guilt you've got to hit."
  • Burnout prevention. Simplify life, address the gap between expectations and capacity, and address the guilt that comes from those expectations.

Conditions you'll see often: hypermobility, MCAS, GI distress, POTS, fibromyalgia and chronic fatigue. Many are diagnoses of exclusion and they often travel with anxiety, overwhelm and low mood. Your lane isn't the medical side. It's the "okay, what now?" after the work-up is done: coping, function, routines and roles.

Don't build a program yet

Do roughly 100 sessions with this group first. You'll find the exact pain points the market actually has, rather than guessing them up front.

When you do go one-to-many

Release it for free, charge for implementation. For example, a 10-minute webinar on the guilt of being a neurodivergent parent while juggling burnout, missed career opportunities, relationships, and the gap between who you were and who you are now.

03

Website & borrowing demand

You've never needed marketing for NDIS, because referrals just came. This new stream needs somewhere people can see what you offer. That's the first job of the website, and version one is live. You don't need to learn web design; that's handled.

  • Pages to add later: an NDIS page and your other services, so people looking for those can find them too.
  • Piggyback on demand that already exists. The women's-health GP clinic you mentioned (peri/menopause focus, struggling to find female-focused clinicians) is ideal. They do the medical work; you're the "what do I do with this now?" Ask them what a referral pathway would look like.
  • GP lunch & learns are cheap at almost any price. I'd gladly spend ~$1,500 to get in front of a room of 10–15 GPs. But know your stuff, because they'll ask detailed questions.
  • Resource pages. Webinar → transcript → its own web page. It's the same approach I use for Estus Health's webinar pages.
  • Hosting: Vercel, which is free. I'll walk you through it.

Telehealth setup = client experience

Telehealth NDIS is the most contested market you'll ever see. Clinicians all over the country say they offer neuroaffirming care for late-diagnosed people. What separates you is onboarding, retention and the quality of the telehealth experience itself. A laptop webcam doesn't cut it.

05

Booking systems

Practice management

A client portal where existing clients can book, cancel and reschedule themselves. New clients are screened and onboarded and sign a service agreement before they get access to self-booking.

Public booking page

A separate Google Calendar (or Calendly) booking page for welcome chats, consulting and supervision. People just drop in. It's linked from your new website.

  • Timing: if you're going to switch practice-management systems, now (while Liz is on maternity leave) is a good window.
  • Online booking forces you to be intentional about availability. It can feel like handing over control, but it's the opposite: you decide when people can reach you.
  • Website: once your Google Calendar appointment schedule is set up, its link goes into one setting and the booking buttons go live.
06

Calendar, blocks & closing loops

I work about 3 hours a day on the business, with roughly 12 sessions a week. I do far less and get more done because I'm not distracted. None of it relies on willpower.

  • Blocks with one life role each. Before 8am, 8–12, 12–4:30 and 7:30–bed. Monday and Tuesday afternoons I'm only a therapist. If I try to work while parenting, I do neither.
  • A day built for interruption. Fridays are my manager and consulting day. On other days you simply can't book a meeting with me.
  • 30-minute buffers between sessions for notes, a snack and the bathroom. That's why I can be fully present in session. Back-to-back driving days leave you shattered, with notes still to do.
  • The horse emoji 🐴. Our team posts it when all notes and invoicing are done. Then you shut down and stop thinking about work. Close the open loops.
  • Deep work means working on the business: SOPs, infrastructure, marketing plans and ideas, uninterrupted. It's separate from admin and client work.
  • Transition routines and state change. Peppermint spray, a diffuser, whatever signals "this is what I'm doing now".
  • Pull, don't push. A 12-month board with one or two things per stretch. Nothing new comes on until something comes off. Each week, manually plan one thing per block.
  • Four-hour blocks feel scary? Make them shorter. The principle matters, not the length.

Environment over willpower

Ask: "What change to my environment would guarantee the result?" If someone shopped, cooked and made your tea every day, you'd get fit. The environment sets up your next action.

The empty Saturday

Waking up to nothing planned feels confronting, even paralysing. That's normal at first. A quieter calendar is the point, not a sign something's wrong.

Marketing & growth: what it really takes

Marketing and acquisition take about half my week. The mindset shift is: "I spend about 3 hours a day on referrals. That's my one job, and it sets everything else up."

12 wksof consistent content before it turns into new referrals
~$65Meta ad cost per new client, vs ~$4,000 annual client value
~96%client retention, with a focus on retention and reactivation
100welcome-call transcripts mined for pain points, turned into playbooks
  • Channels: Instagram, Facebook, YouTube, LinkedIn (company and personal), a podcast, webinars, referral partners and networking groups. Clip long content into shorts.
  • The client journey is the sales process: onboarding → goal setting → goal review at around 6 weeks. When there's good progress four to six weeks in, that's the time to commit for the year, not at plan review.
  • Retention comes from stakeholder engagement. How well do you engage everyone else at the table?
  • Those "40 referrals next week" email programs mostly reactivate an existing database. They suit businesses with lots of lapsed clients (e.g. physios) more than you.
  • Scaling: solo, $200–250k is very doable. Getting to ~$700k means around four more clinicians, all fully booked. That's a supply problem, and it needs systems.
  • Hiring: new grads need a lot of hand-holding. Anyone joining needs to be driven and comfortable working as a fairly solo therapist.
